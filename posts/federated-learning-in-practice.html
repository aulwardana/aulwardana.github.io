Why Every AI Researcher Should Learn Indonesian NLP

The rich morphological structure of Bahasa Indonesia makes it a uniquely challenging and rewarding testbed for modern NLP methods — and an underexplored opportunity for the global research community.

  2. Why Indonesian Is Interesting

Introduction

When researchers talk about low-resource NLP, they often mean languages with fewer than a few hundred thousand training samples. Bahasa Indonesia, with over 270 million speakers and a rapidly growing digital footprint, is not technically "low-resource" in terms of raw data. Yet it remains severely underrepresented in benchmark datasets, pre-trained models, and international publications.

This is a missed opportunity — not just for Indonesian researchers, but for the entire NLP community. Indonesian has structural properties that stress-test modern architectures in ways that English simply cannot, making it an ideal language for probing the limits of current approaches.

Why Indonesian Is Interesting

Unlike English, Indonesian is an agglutinative language — words are formed by combining a root with affixes that carry grammatical meaning. The word mempertanggungjawabkan is a single token composed of four morphemes: a causative prefix, a root, and two suffixes. Tokenizing this correctly requires morphological awareness that most subword tokenizers were not designed for.

"Most tokenizers were designed by English-speaking engineers, for English-speaking use cases. The diversity of world languages demands we revisit these assumptions."

Furthermore, colloquial Indonesian (used in social media, messaging, and everyday speech) diverges significantly from the formal register used in official documents and textbooks. A model trained on formal text will frequently fail on informal inputs, making domain adaptation a first-class problem.

Key Challenges

Three areas deserve particular attention:

  • Morphological complexity: Affixation rules interact with phonological constraints in non-trivial ways.
  • Code-switching: Indonesian speakers frequently mix Bahasa Indonesia with English, Javanese, or local dialects.
  • Benchmark scarcity: High-quality annotated datasets for tasks like NER, coreference, and discourse parsing remain rare.

Current Work and Tools

The situation is improving. Several efforts have produced useful baselines. IndoBERT, released in 2020, provided a pre-trained BERT model for Indonesian. More recently, community-driven projects have produced larger corpora, better tokenizers, and task-specific fine-tuned models.

In our lab, we have been working on IndoNLP Toolkit, an open-source library that consolidates these resources into a single, production-ready pipeline. Here is a simple usage example: 

from indonlp import pipeline

nlp = pipeline("ner", model="indonlp/indobert-base-ner")
result = nlp("Budi Santoso bekerja di Universitas Brawijaya di Malang.")
print(result)
# [{'entity': 'B-PER', 'word': 'Budi'}, {'entity': 'I-PER', 'word': 'Santoso'}, ...]

The library handles tokenization, morphological normalization, and model inference in a single call, making it easy to integrate into downstream applications.

Conclusion

Indonesian NLP is not a niche specialty — it is a proving ground for ideas that will matter across all agglutinative and morphologically rich languages. Researchers who invest in understanding it will find themselves with insights that transfer broadly, and with an opportunity to make an outsized impact in a community that is currently underserved by the tools and benchmarks we take for granted in English.

If you are working on multilingual NLP or low-resource methods, I encourage you to add Indonesian to your evaluation suite. You might be surprised by what you learn.
